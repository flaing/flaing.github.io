To understand how the three modern slogans resonate with the theology of 19th-century Slaveholder Christianity and the early Southern Baptist Convention (SBC), we must look at the exact rhetoric of that era.
The defense of slavery was built upon “Paternalism”—the theological belief that God established a holy hierarchy where the white Christian patriarch (the Master) was appointed by divine right to govern his wife, his children, and his slaves.
Here is how the defenders of slavery argued these exact principles in the English of their time:

I. “MAKE MEN MASCULINE AGAIN”

  • The Modern Premise: Re-establish absolute male headship, rejecting “effeminacy” and egalitarianism, demanding men rule as strong authorities.
  • The 19th-Century Pro-Slavery Rhetoric:
    To the antebellum Southern theologian, true Christian masculinity was defined by a man’s willingness and divine duty to exercise absolute dominion over his household. To surrender authority was seen as unmanly and ungodly.John L. Dagg (1794–1884), one of the early SBC’s most influential theologians, explicitly argued that God ordained this hierarchy of male authority:

“The principles of the Bible, and the laws enacted by Moses, recognized the principle of slavery… The New Testament, so far from condemning slavery, gives explicit directions to masters how to treat their slaves, and commands slaves to be obedient and faithful.”

James Henley Thornwell (1812–1862), a leading Southern Presbyterian divine, argued that any man who abandoned this hierarchical order was committing treason against God’s design:

“The parties in this conflict are not merely Abolitionists and Slaveholders; they are Atheists, Socialists, Communists, Red Republicans, Jacobins on the one side, and the friends of order and regulated liberty on the other.”

II. “MAKE WOMEN FEMININE AGAIN”

  • The Modern Premise: Women must return to their natural state of submission and domesticity, rejecting feminism under the guise of “complementarianism.”
  • The 19th-Century Pro-Slavery Rhetoric:
    Pro-slavery apologists directly linked the “feminine submission” of wives to the “servile submission” of enslaved people. They argued that the Bible commanded both to be under the authority of the patriarch, and to disrupt one was to destroy femininity itself.George Fitzhugh (1806–1881), a leading pro-slavery theorist, wrote extensively on how the “true nature” of women and slaves required them to be ruled by men:

“We do not agree with the authors of the Declaration of Independence, that all men are born free and equal… Men are not born physically, morally or intellectually equal. … So long as she is woman, she must be dependent. She must have a master and protector. … The master occupies towards him [the slave] the place of parent or guardian.”

Even conservative Northern theologians who supported the South’s biblical arguments, like Charles Hodge of Princeton (1797–1878), used the submission of women to justify the subjugation of slaves:

“We believe that the large majority of the human race is, by the law of God, placed under the authority of others. … We deprive the whole female sex of the right of self-government, for the general good.”

III. “MAKE CHILDREN INNOCENT AGAIN”

  • The Modern Premise: Insulate children from progressive ideas and sexual education, maintaining absolute parental control over their innocence.
  • The 19th-Century Pro-Slavery Rhetoric:
    In the antebellum South, the ultimate “paternalistic lie” was that black slaves were mentally and morally equivalent to perpetual children. The slaveholders argued that slaves were simple, innocent, and incapable of self-governance; therefore, keeping them enslaved was a form of Christian “protection” from the corrupting, dangerous ideas of Northern abolitionism.Dr. Samuel Adolphus Cartwright (1793–1863), a Southern physician and pro-slavery advocate, argued that freedom would ruin the “innocent, child-like nature” of the African:

“The negro is a child in intellect, and requires a master to govern him… like children, they require government, and are happier under it than any other.”

Thomas Roderick Dew (1802–1846), President of the College of William & Mary, argued that the hierarchy protected the weak and innocent from a chaotic world:

“The slaveholder is the guardian of the slave… [Slavery] is the order of nature and of God. It is the institution which has achieved the civilization of the world.”

The Ultimate Counter-Argument from the Era

No one understood the hypocrisy of this “protectionist, patriarchal” theology better than those who escaped it. Frederick Douglass (1818–1895), in his famous appendix to his 1845 autobiography, sharply contrasted the pure gospel of Christ with the Slaveholder Christianity that sought to rule over women, men, and children under the guise of holiness:

“I love the pure, peaceable, and impartial Christianity of Christ: I therefore hate the corrupt, slaveholding, women-whipping, cradle-plundering, partial and hypocritical Christianity of this land.”

The three modern slogans—disguised as a return to “traditional values”—utilize the exact same rhetorical scaffolding used 180 years ago: framing subjugation as divine order, and absolute control as paternal protection.
