1. Reclaiming Roman Law to Dismantle Religious Courts

During the Enlightenment and the eras leading up to it, European legal scholars and philosophers sought to weaken the total authority of Church canon law. To do this, they did not invent a brand-new secular system; instead, they explicitly revived pre-Christian Roman law (specifically from the classical Republic and early Empire) to argue that the state’s authority belongs to the civil ruler, not the Church.

  • Separating Crime from Sin: Philosophers like Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and later Voltaire argued that the state should only punish actions that cause tangible harm to citizens or property (civil infractions). They used classical Roman legal frameworks—which focused on property rights, contracts, and civic order—to argue that “heresy,” “blasphemy,” and traditional sexual sins belonged to the realm of individual conscience, not civil punishment.
  • The Napoleonic Code (1804): This culminated in the legal overhauls of the French Revolution and the subsequent Napoleonic Code. The architects of these laws explicitly looked back to the Roman Emperor Justinian’s civil frameworks (stripping away the later Christian theological additions) to create a unified civil code. By focusing strictly on civic contracts, France decriminalized same-sex acts in 1791, a move that spread across Europe as Napoleon conquered territories and implemented this Roman-derived civil system.
